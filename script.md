# A Hole in the Heart — Script

Format: vertical 9:16 · about 8–9 minutes · one calm narrator (NARRATOR ROB) · about 1,150 words

Note on length: at a calm pace a narrator reads about 140–150 words a minute, so 2,500 words would run
about 17 minutes. To stay under 10 minutes, the narration is about 1,150 words.

---

## 1. Narration (word for word)

**NARRATOR ROB:**

*The baby*

1. This is a newborn baby, and inside its chest is a heart with a small hole in it.
2. That sounds frightening.
3. But a hole in the heart is one of the most common things a baby can be born with.
4. And very often, it turns out fine.
5. Let's go inside and see what's going on.

*The heart*

6. Here's the heart, about the size of its owner's fist.
7. It's a pump with four rooms, called chambers.
8. Two on the right, two on the left.
9. The right side collects blood that has used up its oxygen, and sends it to the lungs.
10. The lungs refill it with oxygen.
11. The left side takes that fresh blood back and pushes it out to the whole body.
12. Between the two sides is a wall of muscle, called the septum.
13. Its job is simple: keep the two sides apart.
14. The left side squeezes much harder than the right, because it has to reach every part of the body.
15. So the pressure on the left is higher.
16. Keep that in mind. It matters in a moment.

*A door before birth*

17. Before birth, a baby doesn't breathe air.
18. It gets its oxygen from its mother, through the placenta.
19. So the lungs don't need much blood yet.
20. To skip them, the upper wall has a small natural opening, with a flap like a little door.
21. It's called the foramen ovale.
22. Blood slips through it, from the right side to the left.
23. After birth, the baby takes its first breaths, and the pressures in the heart change.
24. The flap gets pushed shut, and it usually seals over time.
25. In about one in four adults, it never fully seals.
26. Most of them never know, and it causes them no problems.

*A true hole*

27. A true hole is different.
28. It's a gap in the wall that was left open while the heart was being built.
29. If it's in the upper wall, between the two top chambers, doctors call it an atrial septal defect.
30. If it's in the lower wall, between the two pumping chambers, it's a ventricular septal defect.
31. For short, ASD and VSD.

*Causes*

32. So why does this happen?
33. The heart forms in the first weeks of pregnancy, often before a mother knows she's pregnant.
34. As it forms, walls grow inward to split it into four chambers.
35. Sometimes a wall doesn't close all the way.
36. In most cases, nobody can point to a single reason.
37. Genes play a part, and these holes can run in families.
38. They're also more common in babies with certain genetic conditions, such as Down syndrome.
39. Some things during pregnancy can raise the risk of heart defects.
40. Catching rubella, also called German measles, early in pregnancy.
41. Diabetes that isn't well controlled.
42. Smoking, drinking alcohol, and some medicines.
43. But it's worth saying clearly: most of the time, it's not something a parent did wrong.

*How the body reacts*

44. Now, back to that pressure.
45. The left side pushes harder than the right.
46. So when there's a hole, blood leaks through it from left to right.
47. That blood has just come back from the lungs, full of oxygen.
48. And now it gets sent right back to them.
49. The heart ends up pumping the same blood twice.
50. If the hole is small, this barely matters.
51. Many small holes cause no symptoms, and some close on their own as the child grows.
52. If the hole is large, the heart and lungs have to work much harder.
53. A baby may breathe fast, sweat while feeding, tire easily, and gain weight slowly.
54. Feeding is hard work for a baby. With an overworked heart, it's even harder.
55. Some holes aren't found until adulthood, when a person may feel short of breath or tired during exercise.
56. Over many years, a large hole that isn't treated can stretch parts of the heart.
57. It can raise the pressure in the blood vessels of the lungs.
58. And it can upset the heart's rhythm.
59. That's why doctors keep a close eye on larger holes.

*How doctors find it*

60. Often, the first clue is a sound.
61. Through a stethoscope, a doctor may hear a whooshing noise, called a murmur.
62. That can be the sound of blood rushing through the gap.
63. But many murmurs are harmless, so a murmur alone doesn't mean there's a hole.
64. To see inside, doctors use an echocardiogram.
65. It's an ultrasound of the heart, the same kind of scan used during pregnancy.
66. It shows the walls, the hole, and which way the blood is flowing.
67. Some holes are spotted before birth, on a pregnancy scan.
68. An ECG, which records the heart's electrical signals, and a chest X-ray can help fill in the picture.

*How doctors treat it*

69. Treatment depends on the size of the hole, where it is, and how the body is coping.
70. For small holes, the plan is often to wait and watch.
71. Regular check-ups. Regular scans.
72. If a baby is struggling, medicines can help.
73. Some help the body get rid of extra fluid, so the heart and lungs have less to handle.
74. But medicine doesn't close the hole.
75. To close it, there are two main ways.
76. The first one doesn't need opening the chest.
77. A doctor guides a thin, flexible tube, called a catheter, through a blood vessel, usually from the top of the leg, all the way up into the heart.
78. Through that tube travels a small, folded device.
79. When it reaches the hole, it opens on both sides of the wall, like two small discs.
80. Together, they plug the gap.
81. This works for many holes in the upper wall, and some in the lower wall.
82. The second way is open-heart surgery.
83. A heart-lung machine takes over the pumping, so the surgeon can work on a still heart.
84. The hole is closed with stitches, or covered with a small patch.
85. Over the following months, the heart's own tissue grows over the device or the patch.
86. It becomes part of the wall.
87. Most children who have a hole like this repaired grow up to live full, active lives.

*Prevention*

88. Can it be prevented?
89. Not always. Many holes form with no known cause.
90. But some steps lower the risk of birth defects in general.
91. Before pregnancy, make sure you're vaccinated against rubella.
92. Take folic acid before and during early pregnancy, as doctors recommend.
93. If you have diabetes, work with your doctor to keep it under control, before and during pregnancy.
94. Avoid smoking and alcohol.
95. Check any medicines with a doctor or pharmacist.
96. Go to your pregnancy check-ups. They help catch problems early.
97. And if heart defects run in your family, talk to a doctor before you plan a pregnancy.

*Pulling back*

98. So, let's step back.
99. A wall with a gap.
100. Blood taking a shortcut.
101. A heart working a little harder to make up for it.
102. Heard with a stethoscope. Seen with a scan. Closed with a device or a stitch.
103. Here's the heart again, whole.
104. And here's the baby.
105. A small hole. A common problem.
106. And one that doctors today know very well how to treat.

## 4. Style line

A vintage scientific medical illustration of a human heart, fine ink lines, cross-hatching, stippling, detailed anatomical etching, soft smudged graphite shading. Set against a clean, lightly textured cream parchment paper background. A single accent color of glowing neon soft blue is used exclusively to show electrical currents and fluid movement flowing through the valves. Academic textbook aesthetic, minimalist composition, crisp details, high contrast, 8k resolution
